# Introduction to Glaciology and Ice-Flow Modeling

Welcome to the Rice Glaciology group's open textbook. Here, we introduce the physics of glaciers and ice sheets, demonstrate how the physics of ice is encoded in numerical ice flow models like [icepack](https://icepack.github.io/), and walk through several examples of observations of the cryosphere that are actively shaping our understanding of Earth's ice sheets.

The book is aimed at advanced undergraduates and beginning graduate students who have taken some calculus and physics, but who are new to glaciology. We build up the ideas incrementally and try to introduce mathematics and physics using notebooks intended to gamify learning. Much of the material that we present borrows from standard references like *The Physics of Glaciers* {cite}`cuffey2010` and *Physics of Ice* {cite}`petrenko1999`; we point to specific chapters in these texts and other references for readers who want to dive deeper.

## First observers

The scientific study of ice sheets and glaciers is relatively young compared to other earth sciences situated in academic departments, but observations of glaciers and ice sheets are not. In the arctic, Sámi reindeer herders in northern Fennoscandia have maintained a taxonomy that includes dozens of named snow types that distinguih the density, and physical propreties that affect whether animals can graze. In Sámi, there are hundreds of words for snow and verbs to describe how snow builds up (as opposed to how snow falls). *The climate of the arctic is imprinted/informs the functional action of langauge people use to describe snow*. Communities in the Himalaya, Alaska, Greenland and Skandinavia have all similarly monitored, named, and depended on glaciers and snow for water for centuries.

In the southern hemisphere, oral histories of the Māori tribal groups Ngāti Rārua and Te Āti Awa, describe the navigator Hui Te Rangiora who sailed south from the Cook Islands on the vessel Te Ivi o Atea around 650 CE, into waters his crew described as *Te tai-uka-a-pia*, the sea foaming like arrowroot {cite}`wehi2021`. The accounts speak of a white land that was floating, of bare summits piercing the sky, of animals that dive to great depths. These are likely some of the first observations of floe ice, ice bergs, Antarctic ice shelves, and the marine life of the Southern Ocean, made and preserved roughly twelve centuries before the continent entered European maps (come to my office KWG 220 if you want to see a print of Nansen's first map of Antarctica). Ngāi Tahu and other iwi hold these voyaging histories among their treasured knowledge; a carved pou by the Ngāi Tahu artist Fayne Robinson, Te Kaiwhakatere o te Taki, the Navigator of the Heavens, has stood at Scott Base since 2013 with its face turned to the sky that guided those voyages; and Māori scholars today argue for an Antarctic future managed with Indigenous frameworks alongside the science {cite}`wehi2021policy`.

Glaciological inquiry in the academy grew out of early expeditions from nineteenth-century Europe that continue to shape research agendas and access to the continent. Polar field sciences has built a heroic self-image mythologized as the conquest of hostile landscapes. That image hardened into a gatekeeping conflation of scientific ability with outdoorsmanship that has no basis in the physics or the science of glaciers and remains a huge problem in glaciology. As late as 2009, fewer than a fifth of the authors in the field's journals were women, and the first feminist analysis of the field's history and framing {cite}`carey2016`, was met with a lot of internal critiscim. Demographic surveys of major polar collaborations remain overwhelmingly white {cite}`robel2024` while the people most exposed to the cryosphere's changes are disproportionately Indigenous and are rarely at the table when/where the science is framed and shared. Glacier and ice-sheet change (as we will see) has implications for everyone, and therefore everyone should have access to the fundamental prinpials and tools that shape our understanding of ice-sheets and their contributions to sea level. This shared appreciation for how we understand our science in the context of society has informed and motivated our writing. The tools here are deliberately open, and the content editable as part of a philosophy that embraces progressive evaluation of the science, science-history and understanding.

## How the book is organized

The chapters of our book build on one another and are ordered around the ingredients needed to solve equations that describe ice flow. We first develop a fundamental understanding of the water molecule and polycrystalline ice. This discussion builds to the formulation of momentum balance equations in ice-flow models and the diagnostic problem of solving equations for glacier momentum from the glacier's geometry. We then explore climate forcing, and describe the prognostic problem of modeling glacier response forward in time. Labs run throughout the whole book connecting these topics and apply mechanics to real-world glaciers and ice sheets.

**Getting started** introduces icepack, the flow-modeling package built on [Firedrake](https://www.firedrakeproject.org/) {cite}`shapero2021`, and the Docker image that hosts icepack. **The physics of ice** chapter relates the structure and mechanics of the ice molecule to the physical poperties of ice at continuum scales. The next chapter, **Ice deformation and flow**, develops the continuum mechanics associated with stress and strain, uses these concepts and the preceding molecular scale discussion to introduce the nonlinear rheology of ice, the momentum balance and its approximations and demosntrates how one of these approximations (the shallow-shelf apprixmation) is implemented in icepack. **The bed and basal processes** chapter considers the physics of the ice bed interface and develops theory for heat flow, sliding, subglacial hydrology, and erosion beneath glaciers and ice sheets, and the glacial geomorphology that records deglaciation, with a lab comparing friction laws for Thwiates Glacier.  **The surface and surface processes** chapter moves vertically through the ice column to the surface and explores the surface energy balance, snow processes and firn densification. and the surface hydrology that routes meltwater from supraglacial streams through the glacier. The **Mass balance, climate, and prognostic modeling** chapter covers accumulation and ablation, develops the pognostic equations used to predict ice flow, and uses these equations to motivate glacier response theory. The chapter then uses these kinematic relations to understand glacier sensitivity to climate change, ice sheet instabilities, and the climate archive preserved in ice cores. The **Observing ice** chapter concludes as an appendex entry referenced thoughout that demonstrates important cryosphere measurements and how the data can be processed to answer questions discussed in the text. **The wider cryosphere** closes with a discussion of ice of other worlds, and finally sea level and the impacts of the crysphere on people.

## Running the code

The modeling chapters use icepack, which runs on Firedrake. Firedrake can be awkward to install directly, so this book includes a **Docker image** that includes a pre-installed environment with Firedrake, icepack, and JupyterLab. See {doc}`sections/modeling/icepack-intro`.
